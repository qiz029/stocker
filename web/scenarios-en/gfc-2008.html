Historical scenario

2008 · Financial Crisis

2006–2009 · ~815 trading days · 18 tickers · Systemic collapse and the reversal out of despair

What actually happened

In the 2000s, low interest rates and securitization together inflated a US housing bubble: subprime loans were packaged into AAA-rated products and sold around the world. Rising home prices papered over everything — until prices stopped rising.

Subprime blew up in 2007; Bear Stearns was sold in an emergency deal in March 2008; on September 15, 2008, Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy with roughly $639 billion in assets, the largest bankruptcy in US history. AIG was nationalized, money-market funds nearly faced runs, and the financial system came to the edge of a standstill. The S&P 500 fell from 1,565 in October 2007 to 676 on March 9, 2009 — a drawdown of about 57%.

But history turned there too: the $700 billion TARP bailout, zero interest rates and quantitative easing arrived in sequence, and the market bottomed in March 2009 at the peak of despair — then began a bull run lasting more than a decade.

Inside Stocker

This scenario covers late 2006 through 2009: about 815 trading days and 18 aliased tickers. A systemic collapse means there is almost no safe island — but the reversal out of despair is also the most violent. Here, cash flow and the debt ceiling matter more than foresight: leverage can bankrupt you in the crash, or knock you out just before the turn. Only those who survive to the end earn the right to buy the bottom.
