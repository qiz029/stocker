Historical scenario

1987 · Black Monday

1986–1988 · ~756 trading days · 16 tickers · A raging bull, a single-day −22%, and a quiet recovery

What actually happened

From 1982, US stocks ran a five-year bull market. By August 1987 the Dow had reached 2,722, up more than 40% for the year at one point. On the way up, "portfolio insurance" and program trading flourished — strategies that amplified leverage in good times and wired the market to stampede in bad ones.

On Monday, October 19, 1987, the Dow plunged 22.6% in a single session (508 points) — still the largest one-day percentage drop in US market history. There was no internet and no circuit breakers; many people learned what they had lost by phone and television. From the August peak to the October 19 close, the Dow had fallen about 36%.

Unlike 1929, the crash did not drag down the real economy. The Federal Reserve quickly pledged liquidity, the market steadied, and the Dow quietly recovered its losses in about two years.

Inside Stocker

This scenario covers 1986–1988: about 756 trading days and 16 aliased tickers. For most of it, the market is a quiet, relentless bull — quiet enough to make you forget risk exists. On crash day, the next-open execution rule means that if you wait for the news to place your order, you are already too late. The real game is decided before the fall: your position sizing, your leverage, and the cash you kept for the unexpected.
