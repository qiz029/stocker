Historical scenario

2000 · Dot-Com Bubble

1999–2001 · ~752 trading days · 22 tickers · Mania, the peak, and a long bleed-out

What actually happened

In the late 1990s, the commercialization of the internet ignited one of the wildest manias in stock market history. The NASDAQ climbed from under 1,000 in 1995 to a peak of 5,048 on March 10, 2000. "Losing money to buy growth" became the mainstream story — any company with ".com" in its name could double on its first trading day, while profitability was dismissed as an outdated idea.

The turn came from the money supply: the Federal Reserve hiked rates through 1999 and 2000, and the cheap money disappeared. After the March 2000 peak, the bubble popped in slow motion. Star companies burned through their cash in batches: Pets.com announced liquidation just 268 days after its IPO, and Webvan and eToys followed. The NASDAQ slid to about 1,114 in October 2002 — roughly 78% below the peak — and did not reclaim 5,000 until 2015.

Inside Stocker

This scenario spans 1999–2001: about 752 trading days and 22 aliased tickers. The first half is a party where everyone makes money and the news cheers along — the test is whether you dare to leave while the room is still applauding. The second half is a slow grind down, where the urge to buy every dip and the interest ticking on your credit line can both finish you before dawn. And remember: orders fill at the next open, so by the time bad news prints, the price has usually moved.
